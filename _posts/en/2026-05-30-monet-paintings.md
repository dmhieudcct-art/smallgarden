Claude Monet laid the foundation for Impressionism — a movement that broke away from rigid detail to capture moments, light, and feeling. His most famous works are not his most technically perfect, but his most honest to his own vision.

## Impression, Sunrise (1872)

This is the painting that named an entire movement. Depicting dawn over the port of Le Havre, France — loose brushstrokes, colors bleeding into one another, no clear outlines. When it was first exhibited in 1874, critics mocked it: "Impression? It's just an unfinished sketch." Monet kept the name, and history took his side.

## Water Lilies (Série Nymphéas, 1896–1926)

This is Monet's life's work. More than 250 paintings of the same pond in his garden at Giverny — at different times of day, different seasons, different angles. Not because he lacked ideas, but because he understood that light never repeats itself exactly, and every moment deserves its own painting.

His monumental late series — 8 panels arranged in a circle — is now displayed at the Orangerie in Paris. Standing in that room, you are no longer looking at paintings. You are inside the painting.

## Haystacks (1890–1891)

A seemingly mundane subject: haystacks in a field. Monet painted them more than 25 times, simply to record how light shifts from morning to evening, from summer to winter. This is one of the most systematic studies of light in the history of painting — no less rigorous than any scientific experiment.

## Rouen Cathedral Series (1892–1894)

The same logic: a Gothic cathedral, painted more than 30 times under different lighting conditions. Monet rented a room across from the cathedral and painted from the same viewpoint — changing only the time of day and the weather. The stone surface shifts from golden yellow in the morning to cold blue in the late afternoon.

## Closing

Monet teaches us that the same thing seen at different times can be entirely different things. He didn't paint the world — he painted the way we see the world.
